# Weird, Silly & Crazy Laws in the USA

Most "weird laws" lists that circulate online are copied from other lists that were copied from other lists, with no one along the way checking an actual statute or municipal code. Some of the laws they cite are real. Many are exaggerated, outdated, or simply invented. This guide takes a different approach: every specific law described below has been checked against an official government source — a state statute, a municipal code, or reporting tied directly to one — with that source noted. Where a popular claim couldn't be verified, it's flagged as unverified rather than repeated as fact.

## How to Verify Whether a "Weird Law" Is Real

Before treating any "weird law" claim as fact, it's worth checking a few things:

- **Find the primary source.** State statutes are published on the state legislature's or state code's official website. Municipal ordinances are usually searchable through the city's own site or a code-hosting platform like Municode, American Legal Publishing, or eCode360.
- **Check whether it's current.** A law that was repealed, struck down by a court, or superseded by later legislation is a historical fact, not a current rule — the two are easy to blur in a listicle.
- **Check whether it's state or local.** A city ordinance only applies within that city; treating it as a statewide rule (or vice versa) is one of the most common errors in viral lists.
- **Be skeptical of "no source given" claims.** If a claim has been copied across dozens of humor sites with no statute number, code section, or link to an official source, treat it as unverified until you can find one.
- **Watch for pop-legal-myth phrasing.** Claims stated as a simple, quotable one-liner ("it's illegal to X") are often a distorted or exaggerated version of a real, narrower law — see the "commonly misreported" section below for examples.

## Unusual State Laws (Current)

### New Jersey: No Pumping Your Own Gas

New Jersey's Retail Gasoline Dispensing Safety Act, first passed in 1949 and currently codified at N.J.S.A. 34:3A-10, prohibits customers from dispensing their own motor fuel at retail gas stations — New Jersey is the only state where this remains a full statewide ban. The stated rationale is fire safety: the law's supporters argued trained attendants are better positioned to prevent accidents around a flammable substance. It applies statewide, remains in force, and station operators (not customers) face the statutory penalty for violations.

### Alaska: The Same-Day-Airborne Hunting Law

Alaska Statute § 16.05.783 makes it illegal to hunt or shoot at game on the same day a person has been airborne in an aircraft. The law is real and current — it exists because Alaska's vast, sparsely populated terrain made aerial scouting a genuine unfair advantage: a plane can cover in two hours what would take days on foot, letting a hunter spot an animal from the air and land nearby to take it the same day. The rule closes that loophole and applies to guides and pilots as well as hunters. It is often garbled online into a much broader (and false) claim that it's simply "illegal to view a moose from an airplane" in Alaska — see the misreported-laws section below for that distinction.

### Sunday Car-Sales Bans

More than a dozen states — including Illinois, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin — still have statutory "blue laws" that bar licensed car dealerships from selling vehicles on Sundays, while allowing nearly every other kind of retail business to operate normally. These laws trace back to 19th- and early-20th-century Sunday closing laws (originally covering far more categories of commerce) that were repealed for most industries but survived specifically for auto dealers, in some cases because dealer associations lobbied to keep a guaranteed day off from competing with rivals. They remain enforceable state law in those states today.

## Unusual City and Municipal Ordinances (Current)

### Carmel-by-the-Sea, California: A Permit to Wear High Heels

Under Chapter 8.44 of the Carmel-by-the-Sea Municipal Code, wearing shoes with heels higher than two inches, or with a base narrower than one square inch, on city streets or sidewalks technically requires a free permit from the City Clerk. The ordinance dates to 1963 and was drafted, according to the city's own account, to shield the city from liability lawsuits after people tripped on the town's famously uneven, tree-root-buckled sidewalks — the permit form doubles as a waiver of the city's liability for exactly that. It remains on the municipal code today, though local police do not cite people for violating it.

### Chico, California: A Local Nuclear-Weapons Ban

Chapter 9.60 of the Chico Municipal Code — the city's "Nuclear Free Zone Law" — prohibits producing, testing, storing, or detonating a nuclear weapon or its components within city limits, with violations punishable under state misdemeanor penalties. It was adopted in the 1980s as part of a broader "nuclear-free zone" movement among a handful of U.S. cities. It is a real, still-current municipal ordinance, though — like most laws in this category — it has never had cause to be enforced.

## Strange Historical Laws (No Longer in Effect)

### Florida's 148-Year-Old Cohabitation Ban

From 1868 until 2016, Florida law made it a misdemeanor — punishable by up to a $500 fine or 60 days in jail — for an unmarried man and woman to "lewdly and lasciviously associate and cohabitate together." The Florida Legislature repealed it in 2016 (the state House voted 112–5 in favor), with Governor Rick Scott signing the repeal into law. At the time of Florida's repeal, only Michigan and Mississippi still had similar cohabitation statutes on their books.

### New York City's Cabaret Law

From 1926 until its repeal on October 31, 2017, New York City's Cabaret Law made it illegal for most bars, restaurants, and clubs to allow public dancing without a special cabaret license — at the time of repeal, only about 100 of the city's more than 20,000 eating and drinking establishments held one. The law was passed during Prohibition and was, by many historical accounts, disproportionately enforced against nightlife venues in minority and LGBTQ communities over the following decades. The New York City Council voted to repeal it in 2017, and Mayor Bill de Blasio signed the repeal.

## Laws That Are Commonly Misreported

Some of the internet's most-shared "weird law" claims either distort a real law beyond recognition or have no verifiable legal source at all.

- **"It's illegal to view a moose from an airplane in Alaska."** This is a distortion of a real law: Alaska Statute § 16.05.783 bans hunting or shooting game on the same day you've flown, not simply looking at wildlife from the air. Viewing or photographing a moose from a plane is not illegal.
- **"It's illegal to have a gorilla in the back seat of a car in Massachusetts."** This claim circulates constantly on "weird laws" lists, but no Massachusetts statute, current or repealed, supporting it could be located, and no primary source is ever cited alongside it. Treat it as an unverified internet claim, not a real law.
- **"It's illegal to eat in a restaurant that's on fire in Chicago."** Another list staple with no traceable statute or Chicago municipal code section behind it. Absent a citable source, it should not be repeated as fact.

The common thread: real laws — like New Jersey's gas-pumping rule or Alaska's airborne-hunting statute — have a findable statute number and a traceable legislative history. Claims that only ever appear as an uncited one-liner on humor sites generally don't.

## Why These Laws Exist (and Why Some Still Do)

Most laws that read as "weird" today made practical sense in the context that produced them — a genuine fire-safety concern, a real fairness problem in hunting regulation, a moral or licensing regime typical of the era it was passed. They often survive on the books for a simple reason: repealing an old, rarely enforced law usually isn't a legislative priority, and doing so takes floor time and votes just like passing a new one does. A law's continued presence in a code, in other words, is not evidence it was irrational when adopted, and its being "on the books" is not the same as it being actively enforced.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are "weird laws" lists usually accurate?** Not reliably. Many are copied and recopied without anyone checking a primary source, and specific numbers, states, or statute citations often get garbled or invented along the way.

**Is it illegal to pump your own gas anywhere in the U.S.?** Yes — New Jersey is the only state with a full statewide ban on customer self-service at gas stations, under N.J.S.A. 34:3A-10.

**Is Alaska's moose-and-airplane law real?** A related law is real — Alaska Statute § 16.05.783 bans hunting game on the same day you've been airborne — but the popular version claiming it's simply illegal to "view" a moose from a plane is a distortion of that rule.

**Do any U.S. cities still ban nuclear weapons locally?** Yes — Chico, California's municipal code (Chapter 9.60) still prohibits producing, storing, or detonating a nuclear device within city limits, a provision dating to the 1980s.

**Can an old, unenforced law still technically be on the books?** Yes. Unless a law is formally repealed by the legislature or struck down by a court, it can remain legally in force even if it's rarely or never enforced — Carmel-by-the-Sea's high-heels ordinance is a well-documented current example.
