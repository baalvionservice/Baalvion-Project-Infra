# Tender Boat, Excursion & Dinner Cruise Accidents

Not every passenger vessel accident happens aboard a large cruise ship. Tender boats that ferry passengers to shore, organized shore excursions, and standalone dinner or sightseeing cruises each involve their own vessels, operators, and liability questions. This guide explains how these accidents are generally analyzed. It is general legal information, not legal advice about any specific injury.

## Tender Boat Accidents

When a cruise ship anchors offshore rather than docking directly at a port, passengers are often transported to shore on smaller vessels called tenders — sometimes operated by the cruise line itself, and sometimes by a local third-party company. Tender boat accidents can include falls during boarding or disembarking (particularly in rough water, where the tender and gangway can move independently), collisions, and overcrowding-related incidents. Whether the cruise line or a separate tender operator bears responsibility often depends on who owned and operated the tender and what safety procedures were in place for boarding and disembarking.

## Shore Excursion Accidents

Shore excursions — everything from guided tours and snorkeling trips to zipline and ATV excursions — are frequently operated by independent local companies rather than the cruise line itself, even when booked directly through the cruise line's own excursion desk or website. This distinction matters because cruise lines often argue they are not liable for an independent contractor's negligence during an excursion. That said, a cruise line may still face potential liability in certain circumstances, including:

- **Negligent selection** — if the cruise line failed to exercise reasonable care in choosing or vetting the excursion operator
- **Apparent agency** — if the excursion was marketed and sold in a way that reasonably led passengers to believe the operator was part of the cruise line's own service, rather than an independent company
- **Direct cruise line negligence** — if cruise line staff, such as those coordinating embarkation for the excursion, contributed to the accident

## Dinner Cruises and Sightseeing Cruises

Standalone dinner cruises, harbor sightseeing cruises, and similar short passenger vessel outings are typically operated by local companies entirely separate from the major cruise lines, and are not covered by a cruise line's ticket contract at all. These outings are still generally governed by maritime law if they occur on navigable water, meaning the operator owes passengers a duty of reasonable care, but the specific notice and filing deadlines that apply to major cruise line ticket contracts under 46 U.S.C. § 30508 may not apply in the same way — the terms depend on the specific ticket or booking agreement for that outing, which should be reviewed directly.

## Common Causes of These Accidents

Accidents connected to tenders, excursions, and smaller passenger vessels commonly involve slippery boarding areas, inadequate lighting, insufficient crew assistance during boarding, overcrowding, equipment failure, and, for excursions involving activities like snorkeling or ziplining, inadequate safety briefing or equipment. Weather and water conditions can also play a significant role, particularly for tender operations at anchorages without a sheltered harbor.

## Who May Be Liable

Depending on the specific facts, potentially liable parties can include the cruise line, an independent tender or excursion operator, the owner of a standalone dinner or sightseeing vessel, and, in some cases, an equipment manufacturer if defective gear contributed to the accident. Identifying the correct party — and the correct contract or ticket terms that apply to that specific portion of the trip — is often one of the more complex parts of these claims.

## What to Do After an Accident

As with any maritime accident, seeking prompt medical care, reporting the incident to whichever staff or crew were present (cruise line, tender operator, or excursion company), and gathering photographs, witness information, and any available incident report are generally important steps. Because responsibility can be split between the cruise line and an independent operator, it's also useful to document exactly who was operating the vessel or leading the excursion at the time of the accident.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If I booked an excursion through the cruise line, is the cruise line automatically responsible?** Not automatically — many excursions are run by independent local operators, and liability depends on factors like how the excursion was marketed, whether the cruise line vetted the operator reasonably, and who actually caused the accident.

**Does the cruise line's ticket contract cover a standalone dinner cruise I booked separately?** Generally no — a separately booked local dinner or sightseeing cruise is typically governed by its own booking terms, not the cruise line's passenger ticket contract.

**What if the tender boat accident happened during boarding, before I was technically off the ship?** This can affect which vessel and which operator's duties are most directly relevant; a maritime injury lawyer can help sort out the specific facts and applicable duties.
