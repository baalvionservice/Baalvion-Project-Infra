# How Trademark Disputes Over Runway Collection Names Are Resolved

A collection or line name is one of the first things a fashion house registers, and one of the first things it fights over. When two brands land on similar names for a runway line, a capsule collection, or a signature silhouette, the dispute usually turns on a small set of recurring legal questions rather than on who used the name "first" in a casual sense. This guide explains, in general terms, how those disputes are typically analyzed. It is general legal information, not legal advice about any specific brand or dispute.

## Trademark Rights Versus Just "Using a Name"

In the United States, trademark rights in fashion generally arise from actual use of a mark in commerce, not merely from registration — though federal registration with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) gives a brand significant additional advantages, including a legal presumption of ownership and nationwide priority. A designer who has used a collection name in commerce, even unregistered, can still have enforceable common-law trademark rights in the geographic area where that use occurred.

## Likelihood of Confusion: The Core Test

Most trademark disputes, including those over collection or line names, are resolved by asking whether an ordinary consumer would likely be confused about the source of the goods. Courts and the USPTO typically weigh factors such as: the similarity of the marks in sound, appearance, and meaning; the similarity of the goods (a "confusion" case is much stronger between two apparel lines than between apparel and an unrelated product category); the strength of the senior mark; evidence of actual confusion; and whether the marks are sold through overlapping channels to overlapping customers. No single factor is automatically decisive — the analysis is holistic.

## Trade Dress: Protecting Look, Not Just Names

Fashion disputes frequently extend beyond word marks into trade dress — the overall look, packaging, or distinctive design elements of a product line. The Second Circuit's decision in *Christian Louboutin S.A. v. Yves Saint Laurent America, Inc.* is a widely cited example: the court held that Louboutin's red-lacquered outsole had acquired distinctiveness and was protectable trade dress, but narrowed that protection to contrasting-color soles, since YSL's monochrome red shoe (sole and upper both red) did not infringe. The case illustrates both how far trade dress protection can reach in fashion and how narrowly courts can construe it once functionality and industry practice are considered.

## Functionality and Aesthetic Choices

A recurring defense in fashion trade dress cases is functionality — a design feature is not protectable trademark subject matter if it is functional, meaning it affects the cost or quality of the product or is essential to its use. Courts also consider "aesthetic functionality," asking whether protecting a design feature would put competitors at a significant non-reputation-related disadvantage. This defense is one reason many fashion design disputes are fought under copyright or design patent law instead of, or alongside, trademark claims.

## Descriptive and Generic Collection Names

Collection names built from ordinary descriptive words (a season, a fabric, a color, a silhouette type) face a harder path to trademark protection than coined or arbitrary names. A descriptive name can still be protected once it has acquired "secondary meaning" — consumers associating it specifically with one brand — but a name found to be generic for the type of product cannot be protected at all, regardless of how long it has been used.

## Concurrent Use and Geographic Limits

Because common-law rights are tied to actual use in a geographic market, it is possible for two unrelated brands to hold legitimate rights to similar names in different regions, particularly before either seeks federal registration. Federal registration significantly narrows this possibility going forward by establishing nationwide constructive use, which is one of the primary reasons fashion houses register collection and house names as early as commercially practical.

## How These Disputes Typically Resolve

Most collection-name conflicts are resolved well short of trial: through a cease-and-desist letter and negotiated rebrand, a coexistence agreement limiting each party's use to specific channels or markets, or a licensing arrangement. Litigation and formal opposition or cancellation proceedings before the USPTO's Trademark Trial and Appeal Board are typically reserved for disputes where the parties cannot agree on scope, or where one side believes a rebrand would cause significant commercial harm.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a fashion brand trademark a color?** Yes, in limited circumstances — a color can be protected as trade dress once it has acquired distinctiveness and is not functional, as in the Louboutin red-sole case, but courts apply this narrowly.

**Does using a name first always win a dispute?** Not automatically. Priority of use matters, but the outcome also depends on likelihood of confusion, the strength and distinctiveness of the mark, and whether either side has secured federal registration.

**Is a runway show name automatically trademarked?** No. Trademark rights require use of the name in commerce in connection with goods or services, not simply announcing or showing a collection under that name.
