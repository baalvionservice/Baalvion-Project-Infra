# White-Collar Investigations: What to Expect

Most white-collar investigations don't begin with an arrest — they begin quietly, with a document preservation notice or a subpoena, often addressed to a company rather than an individual. By the time an investigation becomes public, it has usually been running for months. This guide walks through how a U.S. federal white-collar investigation typically unfolds and why the earliest response often shapes the outcome more than anything that happens later. It describes the U.S. federal process specifically; state and non-U.S. systems follow different procedures. This is general legal information, not legal advice about any specific matter.

## The Early Signals

The first sign of an investigation is often one of two things: a **document preservation letter**, asking the recipient to stop routine deletion of records related to a specific matter, or a **subpoena**, a compulsory demand for documents (a subpoena duces tecum) or testimony (a subpoena ad testificandum), typically issued through a federal grand jury. Preservation obligations attach as soon as litigation or an investigation is reasonably anticipated — destroying, altering, or concealing records at that point can itself become a separate federal crime under obstruction statutes such as 18 U.S.C. § 1519, regardless of what the underlying investigation was originally about. This is one of the most consequential and most common mistakes people make early in an investigation.

## Target, Subject, and Witness

In a federal investigation, prosecutors generally place each person or company involved into one of three categories, and the classification affects how the investigation is likely to proceed for that person:

- **Target** — someone the prosecutor has substantial evidence linking to a crime and who is a putative defendant.
- **Subject** — someone whose conduct is within the scope of the investigation but who has not yet been identified as a target.
- **Witness** — someone believed to have relevant information but who is not, at least currently, suspected of wrongdoing.

These categories can and do change as an investigation develops — a subject can become a target, and a witness can become a subject. Prosecutors are not always required to disclose which category someone falls into, though a "target letter" is sometimes sent notifying a person they are a target and, commonly, inviting them to testify before the grand jury or negotiate. Anyone contacted by investigators, even informally, should generally assume their status could shift and respond accordingly.

## The Role of the Grand Jury

Serious federal white-collar cases are typically investigated through a **grand jury** — a body of citizens with the power to subpoena documents and testimony and, ultimately, to vote on whether there is probable cause to issue an indictment. Grand jury proceedings are conducted under strict secrecy rules (Fed. R. Crim. P. 6(e)), and a subpoenaed witness generally does not have a right to have their own lawyer present in the grand jury room, though they can typically step outside to consult counsel during questioning. Receiving a grand jury subpoena — whether for documents or testimony — is itself a serious event that generally warrants involving counsel before responding, not after.

## Internal Investigations and Privilege

Companies facing a government inquiry commonly conduct their own internal investigation in parallel, often through outside counsel, both to understand what happened and to be positioned to respond credibly to investigators. Communications with counsel conducted for the purpose of obtaining legal advice are typically protected by attorney-client privilege, and materials prepared in anticipation of litigation may be protected by the work-product doctrine — but both protections can be lost through careless handling, such as forwarding privileged communications to non-lawyers or failing to mark and segregate privileged materials. How an internal investigation is structured, and who it ultimately reports to, materially affects whether these protections hold up.

## Parallel Civil and Criminal Proceedings

Many white-collar matters generate both a criminal investigation and a related civil or regulatory proceeding — for example, a criminal fraud investigation running alongside an SEC enforcement action, or a criminal tax matter running alongside a civil IRS audit. These parallel tracks raise their own strategic questions, including how information produced in one proceeding might be used in the other, and whether to assert Fifth Amendment protections in a civil deposition while a related criminal matter is pending.

## Cooperation, Proffers, and Charging Decisions

As an investigation matures, a subject or target's counsel may negotiate a **proffer session** — a structured, typically limited-immunity interview where the person shares information with prosecutors, often to explore a cooperation agreement or a more favorable resolution. Whether and how to engage in this process depends heavily on the specific facts and the person's actual exposure, and prosecutors' formal charging decisions in federal cases are guided by written principles, including the Justice Manual's Principles of Federal Prosecution. Not every investigation ends in charges — many close without any action, particularly where evidence doesn't ultimately support the initial concern.

## Why Early Counsel Matters

The choices made in the first weeks of an investigation — what gets preserved, what gets said informally to investigators, whether and how a company self-reports, how an internal investigation is scoped — are difficult or impossible to undo later, and often do more to shape the outcome than anything that happens after charges (if any) are filed. This is the practical reason experienced white-collar counsel consistently advise getting involved as soon as a preservation notice or subpoena arrives, rather than waiting to see how serious the matter becomes.
