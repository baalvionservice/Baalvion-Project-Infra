# Textile and Apparel Supply Chain Disclosure Laws Explained

Fashion and apparel supply chains are famously long and fragmented — raw fiber, spinning, weaving or knitting, dyeing, cut-and-sew, and finishing can each happen in a different country before a garment ever reaches a retailer. A growing body of law requires brands to disclose what they know (and are doing) about labor and sourcing practices across that chain, and in some cases bars import of goods made with forced labor outright. This guide explains, in general terms, the main U.S. laws in this area. It is general legal information, not legal advice about a specific brand's compliance obligations.

## California's Transparency in Supply Chains Act

The California Transparency in Supply Chains Act (SB 657), which took effect in 2012, requires large retailers and manufacturers doing business in California — generally those with worldwide gross receipts above a statutory threshold — to publicly disclose their efforts, if any, to eradicate human trafficking and slavery from their direct supply chains. The law does not mandate that a company take any specific action against trafficking; it mandates disclosure of whatever the company does or does not do, across five specific categories: verification of supply chains for trafficking risk, supplier audits, certification from direct suppliers of legal compliance, maintenance of internal accountability standards, and employee/management training. A company can technically comply by disclosing that it does none of these — the law's mechanism is public transparency and reputational pressure rather than a substantive labor standard.

## The Federal Forced-Labor Import Ban

Separate from disclosure requirements, Section 307 of the Tariff Act of 1930 (19 U.S.C. § 1307) prohibits the importation of goods made wholly or in part with forced labor, including convict labor, forced child labor, and indentured labor. U.S. Customs and Border Protection enforces this through Withhold Release Orders (WROs) and formal findings against specific producers, regions, or supply chains, which can result in detention or exclusion of shipments believed to be tainted by forced labor, placing the burden on the importer to prove otherwise.

## The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act

The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA), effective from 2022, created a rebuttable presumption that goods mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part in China's Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region — or made by certain listed entities — are made with forced labor and are therefore barred from importation, unless the importer can provide clear and convincing evidence otherwise and demonstrate full compliance with CBP's guidance. Because cotton and other textile inputs from Xinjiang have historically supplied a meaningful share of global apparel manufacturing, UFLPA enforcement has had a significant, direct effect on fashion and apparel supply chains, requiring far more granular supply chain mapping — down to raw material origin — than many brands previously maintained.

## State-Level Disclosure Beyond California

Following California's lead, other jurisdictions have considered or adopted their own supply chain disclosure requirements, and some states have incorporated forced-labor and human trafficking disclosure into public procurement rules, requiring vendors selling to state government to make similar certifications. Brands operating nationally generally need to track this landscape by state rather than assuming California's rule is the only one that applies to them.

## International Comparisons: The UK and EU Approach

Outside the U.S., the UK's Modern Slavery Act 2015 imposes a broadly similar disclosure obligation on larger companies doing business in the UK, while more recent EU measures — including corporate sustainability due diligence requirements adopted at the EU level — go further in some respects by requiring companies to actively identify and address human rights and environmental risks in their supply chains, not merely disclose what they do or don't do. A fashion brand selling across multiple markets increasingly has to satisfy several different regulatory approaches simultaneously.

## What Compliance Actually Involves in Practice

Meeting these obligations in practice typically involves supply chain mapping well beyond first-tier (direct) suppliers to sub-tier fabric mills, dye houses, and raw material sources; supplier codes of conduct with audit rights; third-party social compliance audits; documentation systems capable of producing traceability evidence quickly if a shipment is detained; and, for the disclosure-only statutes, accurate and specific public reporting rather than generic boilerplate language, which regulators and advocacy groups have both criticized as inadequate in some companies' early disclosures.

## Enforcement and Practical Risk

California's disclosure law is enforceable by the state Attorney General through injunctive relief for non-compliant disclosure, though private lawsuits under the statute itself are limited; much of the practical pressure instead comes from investor, NGO, and media scrutiny of what companies actually disclose. The federal forced-labor import rules carry more direct commercial consequences — detained or excluded shipments, which can be commercially severe for time-sensitive fashion goods — making supply chain traceability as much a business continuity issue as a legal compliance one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does California's law require brands to eliminate forced labor from their supply chain?** No — it requires disclosure of what efforts, if any, a covered company makes; it does not itself mandate a specific substantive standard.

**Can a shipment be blocked from entering the U.S. over forced-labor concerns without a criminal conviction anywhere?** Yes — CBP's Withhold Release Order and UFLPA mechanisms operate administratively, based on CBP's own findings, and do not require a criminal conviction.

**Does UFLPA only affect companies that source directly from Xinjiang?** No — because of how deeply integrated cotton and other textile inputs from the region can be in global supply chains, the presumption can reach goods with no direct, obvious connection to the region unless the importer can trace and document otherwise.
