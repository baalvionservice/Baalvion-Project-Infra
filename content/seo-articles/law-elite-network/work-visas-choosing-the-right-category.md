# Work Visas: Choosing the Right Category

There is no single "work visa" — the U.S. immigration system has a range of employment-based nonimmigrant categories, each tied to a different kind of role, employer relationship, or professional background, and choosing the wrong one can cost months of preparation time. This guide gives a general overview of the main categories employers and prospective employees encounter, how they differ, and how a temporary work visa can potentially lead toward permanent residence. It covers the U.S. system specifically; other countries have entirely different visa frameworks. This is general information, not legal or immigration advice about any specific situation.

## The Common Thread: Employer Sponsorship

Most U.S. employment-based nonimmigrant visas require an employer to sponsor the worker — meaning the employer, not the individual, generally files the initial petition with U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), and the visa is typically tied to continued employment with that sponsoring employer (with some categories allowing more flexibility than others). This is a fundamentally different starting point from a tourist or student visa, and it's why the employer's role in the process — including its size, its ability to demonstrate the position's requirements, and its willingness to handle the paperwork and cost — matters as much as the individual candidate's qualifications.

## The Main Employment-Based Categories

- **H-1B (Specialty Occupation)** — for roles that require at least a bachelor's degree (or equivalent) in a specific specialty field, commonly used for technology, engineering, finance, and other degree-requiring professional roles. The regular H-1B category is subject to an annual numerical cap set by statute (8 U.S.C. § 1184(g)) — historically 65,000 visas, plus an additional 20,000 for individuals with a U.S. master's degree or higher — and when demand exceeds the cap, USCIS runs a random selection process. Certain employers, including universities and some nonprofit and government research organizations, are exempt from the cap.
- **L-1 (Intracompany Transferee)** — for employees of a multinational company being transferred to a U.S. office, branch, subsidiary, or affiliate, generally requiring at least one year of employment abroad with the company in the prior three years. L-1A covers managers and executives; L-1B covers employees with specialized knowledge of the company's products, processes, or procedures.
- **O-1 (Extraordinary Ability)** — for individuals with extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics, demonstrated through sustained national or international acclaim and extensive documentary evidence. It has no annual numerical cap, but the evidentiary bar is intentionally high.
- **TN (USMCA Professionals)** — available to Canadian and Mexican citizens working in specific professional occupations listed under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement, generally a faster and less document-intensive process than H-1B for the occupations it covers.
- **E-2/E-3 (Treaty Investor / Treaty Trader / Australian Specialty)** — E-2 is available to nationals of countries with a qualifying treaty with the U.S. who are making a substantial investment in a U.S. business; E-3 is a category specifically for Australian nationals in specialty occupations, functioning similarly to H-1B but with its own, separate annual allocation.

Other categories exist for specific situations — seasonal or temporary non-agricultural work (H-2B), agricultural work (H-2A), religious workers, and others — and the right category always depends on the specific role, the worker's background, and the employer relationship.

## Matching the Category to the Situation

Choosing among these categories generally depends on three things: **the role's requirements** (does it require a specific degree, extraordinary ability, or fall under a treaty/trade agreement), **the employer relationship** (is this a new U.S. employer, or a transfer within a multinational company), and **timeline** (categories like the H-1B cap process run on an annual cycle, while others, like L-1 or O-1, can generally be filed and processed without waiting for a specific window). Getting this wrong — for example, pursuing an H-1B for a role that doesn't clearly require a specialty degree — can result in a denial that costs both time and filing fees, which is why employers and candidates typically work through this analysis with immigration counsel before filing anything.

## From a Work Visa Toward Permanent Residence

Many employment-based nonimmigrant visas are "dual intent" or otherwise compatible with also pursuing a green card (lawful permanent residence) — H-1B and L-1 are the most common example — meaning the worker can hold the temporary visa while a separate, longer permanent-residence process moves forward. The typical employment-based green card path for many roles starts with a **PERM labor certification** through the U.S. Department of Labor, in which the employer must generally test the U.S. labor market and confirm no qualified, willing U.S. worker is available for the role, followed by an I-140 immigrant petition with USCIS and, ultimately, an application to adjust status (or consular processing, for applicants outside the U.S.). Certain categories — including many O-1 holders with extraordinary ability, and some multinational executives and managers already on L-1A status — may qualify for green card categories that don't require PERM labor certification. Processing times for every stage of this process vary significantly by category, country of birth (due to per-country annual limits on immigrant visas), and current government workload, and change often enough that this guide does not attempt to state current wait times — check USCIS's own processing time data for current figures.
