# Model Agency Licensing Rules — What Varies by State

Unlike most consumer-facing businesses, talent and model agencies in the United States are regulated at the state level, and the rules differ enough that an agency compliant in one state can be operating unlawfully in another. This guide explains, in general terms, how state talent agency licensing typically works and where the biggest variations show up. It is general legal information, not legal advice about a specific agency or jurisdiction.

## Why Talent Agency Regulation Exists

Talent and model agency statutes generally trace back to early-20th-century concerns about exploitative booking practices — agents taking excessive fees, steering talent toward unsafe or fraudulent engagements, or acting simultaneously as both agent and employer in ways that created conflicts of interest. Modern statutes are built around licensing, bonding, and fee-limit requirements meant to address those same underlying risks, even though the entertainment and modeling industries have changed substantially since most of these laws were first written.

## Licensing: Who Actually Needs One

Most states with talent agency statutes require a license for anyone who procures employment for talent (including models) in exchange for a fee, though the exact definition of a regulated "talent agency" varies. Some states carve out an explicit exception or a separate, lighter registration category for "personal managers" who provide career guidance and development without directly procuring bookings — a distinction that has generated significant litigation over the years, since the line between "managing" and "procuring employment" is not always clear in practice.

## Where Licensing Requirements Diverge Most

- **California** — regulated under the Talent Agencies Act, administered by the Labor Commissioner. California requires a license, a surety bond, and mandates that a licensed agency's contracts with artists be filed with the state; unlicensed procurement of employment can render the agency's contracts void and unenforceable, which is a significant enforcement mechanism in disputes.
- **New York** — regulated under the General Business Law's employment agency provisions, administered by the Department of Consumer Affairs in New York City for agencies operating there, with licensing, bonding, and specific recordkeeping requirements.
- **Other states** — many states have their own talent or employment agency statutes with materially different license categories, bonding amounts, and fee caps; several states have no dedicated talent agency statute at all, in which case general employment agency or business licensing law may apply instead, if anything does.

Because of this patchwork, an agency operating across multiple states — increasingly common given how much booking now happens remotely — generally needs to confirm licensing status separately in each state where it actively procures work for talent, not just where it is headquartered.

## Bonding and Trust Account Requirements

States that license talent agencies commonly require a surety bond, intended to provide a source of recovery for a model or performer harmed by an agency's misconduct, and in some states — California among them — a trust account requirement for funds received on a client's behalf, so that talent payments are not commingled with the agency's general operating funds.

## Fee Caps and Commission Limits

A number of states cap the commission a licensed talent agency may charge, commonly in a range historically around 10-20% depending on the state and the type of engagement, though some states leave commission rates to private agreement between the agency and the talent, subject to general contract law and unconscionability principles rather than a hard statutory cap. Where a cap applies, an agreement purporting to charge above it is typically unenforceable to the extent it exceeds the statutory limit.

## Personal Manager vs. Talent Agent Distinction

Because personal managers are typically far less regulated than licensed agents, disputes sometimes arise over whether a given manager crossed the line into unlicensed talent agency activity — for example, by directly negotiating and securing a specific booking rather than only providing general career guidance. In states with strict talent agency statutes, a finding that a manager acted as an unlicensed agent can void the underlying management agreement and forfeit the manager's right to commissions from that engagement, which is why the distinction is taken seriously in entertainment and modeling industry contracts.

## Model-Specific Considerations

Because much of the modeling industry operates through international agency networks and cross-border bookings, a model working in multiple markets may be represented by differently regulated entities in each — a U.S.-licensed talent agency in one market and an unlicensed or differently regulated booking representative abroad. Reviewing how each representing entity is actually licensed in its home jurisdiction, rather than assuming uniform regulation, is a recurring practical issue for models and their advisors.

## What Happens When an Agency Operates Without a Required License

Consequences for operating as an unlicensed talent agency where a license is required vary by state, but can include the agency's contracts being void or voidable, civil penalties, an inability to sue to collect commissions, and in some states, criminal liability for repeated or knowing violations. For a model or performer, this means that confirming an agency's licensing status is not just a formality — it can directly affect whether a booking contract or commission agreement is even enforceable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do all states require talent agencies to be licensed?** No — requirements vary significantly, and some states have no dedicated talent agency licensing statute at all.

**Can an unlicensed agency still collect commissions?** In states with strict statutes like California, an unlicensed agency's procurement contracts can be void, which has historically made commission collection difficult or impossible for unlicensed activity.

**Is a personal manager the same as a licensed talent agent?** No — the two roles are typically regulated differently, though a manager who crosses into procuring employment directly can risk being treated as an unlicensed agent in some states.
