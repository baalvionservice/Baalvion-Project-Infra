# How to Choose a Personal Injury Lawyer

Choosing a personal injury lawyer is a practical decision, not just an emotional one. The right fit depends on the type of case, the lawyer's relevant experience, how they communicate, how their fees are structured, and how comfortable a client feels working with them over what can be a lengthy process. This guide walks through the factors worth evaluating and the questions worth asking before hiring anyone. It does not recommend or endorse any specific lawyer or firm.

## Relevant Experience

Experience matters most when it lines up with the type of case involved. A lawyer with a strong general injury practice may not have deep, specific experience with a maritime injury, cruise ship, or offshore claim, which can involve federal statutes and procedural rules that differ substantially from an ordinary car accident case. Asking directly about experience with the specific type of incident involved — not just personal injury generally — is one of the most useful early questions.

## Practice-Area Specialization

Some lawyers and firms focus heavily on a narrow set of case types (for example, maritime and offshore injury law, or motor vehicle accidents), while others handle a broader range of personal injury matters. Neither approach is inherently better, but a case involving specialized law — such as the Jones Act, general maritime law, or a cruise line's ticket contract — often benefits from a lawyer who regularly works in that specific area, since these cases can involve rules and deadlines that are easy to miss without that background.

## Experience With Similar Cases

Beyond general practice area, it can help to ask how many cases similar in type and severity to yours the lawyer has actually handled, and how those cases were typically resolved — through negotiation, litigation, or both. This is different from asking about overall years in practice, which does not by itself indicate relevant hands-on experience.

## Geographic and Jurisdictional Experience

Personal injury law varies by state, and maritime and admiralty matters can involve federal court and specific jurisdictional rules. A lawyer familiar with the courts, procedures, and legal standards in the relevant jurisdiction — including any state-specific filing deadlines or damages rules — can often navigate a case more efficiently than one encountering that jurisdiction for the first time.

## Communication

Ask how the lawyer or their team typically keeps clients updated, how quickly they generally respond to questions, and who the day-to-day point of contact will be. Cases can take months or longer to resolve, and clear, consistent communication is one of the most commonly cited sources of client frustration or satisfaction with legal representation.

## Availability

A lawyer's caseload can affect how much individual attention a case receives. It is reasonable to ask how a firm manages caseloads and who specifically will be working on the case day to day, especially at larger firms where the lawyer conducting the initial consultation may not be the one handling the case afterward.

## Case-Management Process

Ask what the general process looks like from intake through resolution — how the investigation is handled, how often the client can expect updates, and what the client is expected to do at each stage. A lawyer who can clearly explain their process, without guaranteeing a specific outcome, is generally a good sign.

## Trial Experience

Most personal injury cases settle before trial, but a lawyer's willingness and ability to take a case to trial, if necessary, can affect how seriously an insurer negotiates. It is reasonable to ask about a lawyer's trial experience and how they decide when litigation, rather than continued negotiation, makes sense.

## Settlement Experience

Because most cases resolve through settlement, it is equally reasonable to ask how a lawyer approaches negotiation — how they build a demand, how they typically respond to lowball offers, and how they decide when an offer is fair versus when further negotiation or litigation is warranted.

## Fee Structure

Nearly all personal injury lawyers work on a contingency-fee basis, but the specific percentage and terms vary. See [Contingency Fee Agreements: How Lawyers Get Paid](/contingency-fee-agreements-explained/) for a full explanation of how these arrangements typically work and what to look for.

## Costs and Expenses

Separate from the attorney's fee, ask how case-related costs and expenses are handled, including what happens to those costs if the case is unsuccessful. This is a frequently overlooked detail that can matter significantly depending on the outcome of the case.

## Reviews and Reputation

Client reviews, peer recognition, and bar association standing can provide some context, but should be weighed critically — reviews can be selective, and marketing claims about awards or rankings vary widely in how they are earned. A pattern of consistent, specific feedback (not just a star rating) is generally more informative than a single review or a general claim of being "top rated."

## Professional Credentials

Confirm that a lawyer is licensed and in good standing in the relevant state, which is generally verifiable through that state's bar association. Additional credentials — such as board certification in a specialty area, where a state offers it — can be relevant but are not the only indicator of a lawyer's suitability for a specific case.

## Questions to Ask During a Consultation

- Have you handled cases like mine before, and what were the general outcomes?
- Who will actually be working on my case day to day?
- What do you see as the main challenges in my case?
- How do you structure your contingency fee, and how are expenses handled?
- How will you keep me updated, and how quickly do you typically respond?
- Do you anticipate this case settling, or is litigation likely?

## Warning Signs to Consider

Some signals are worth paying attention to during the evaluation process: pressure to sign immediately without time to review the fee agreement, guarantees of a specific dollar outcome, reluctance to explain how fees and expenses work, or a lack of clarity about who will actually handle the case. None of these alone is necessarily disqualifying, but they are worth asking follow-up questions about.

## How to Compare Multiple Lawyers

When speaking with more than one lawyer, use the same set of questions for each so the answers are genuinely comparable. Pay attention not just to what is said, but to how clearly and specifically each lawyer answers — vague reassurance is less useful than a concrete explanation of process, experience, and fee terms.

## Documents to Bring to an Initial Consultation

- Any accident, incident, or police report already filed
- Photographs or video related to the incident
- Contact information for witnesses
- Insurance information for all relevant parties
- Medical records or provider names for treatment received so far
- Any correspondence already exchanged with an insurance company
- Pay stubs or other documentation of lost income, if applicable

Not having every item should not discourage someone from scheduling a consultation — a lawyer can help identify and gather what is missing.

## When a Second Opinion May Make Sense

Getting a second opinion can be reasonable when a case has already been declined by one lawyer, when a settlement offer seems low relative to the injury involved, when communication with current counsel has broken down, or simply when comparing options before committing to representation. Many personal injury consultations are free, which makes comparing more than one lawyer a low-cost way to make a more informed decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is the most expensive-looking firm the best choice?** Not necessarily. Marketing spend and firm size do not automatically correlate with the quality of representation for a specific case. Relevant experience, communication, and a clear fee agreement matter more than a firm's advertising presence.

**Should I choose a specialist or a general personal injury lawyer?** It depends on the case. Cases involving maritime, offshore, or cruise ship law often benefit from specific experience in those areas; more typical claims, such as a straightforward car accident, may be well handled by a general personal injury practice.

**Can I switch lawyers if I'm not satisfied?** Generally, yes, though doing so can involve fee and logistical considerations that should be understood before switching. This is worth discussing directly with both the current and prospective lawyer.

**Do I have to hire the first lawyer I speak with?** No. Consulting with more than one lawyer before deciding is common and reasonable, particularly for significant injuries.

**How much does an initial consultation cost?** Many personal injury lawyers offer free initial consultations, though this varies by firm and should be confirmed when scheduling.
