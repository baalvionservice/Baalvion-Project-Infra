# Counterfeit Goods Enforcement at Border Customs Explained

Fashion brands lose significant revenue every year to counterfeit goods, and a large share of enforcement against those goods happens not in a courtroom but at a port of entry, before merchandise ever reaches a store shelf or marketplace listing. This guide explains, in general terms, how U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) identifies and acts against counterfeit fashion imports, and what brands can do to strengthen that enforcement. It is general legal information, not legal advice about a specific shipment, seizure, or enforcement action.

## The Legal Basis for Border Seizure

CBP's authority to seize counterfeit and infringing goods at the border rests on several overlapping statutes, most notably Section 526 of the Tariff Act (19 U.S.C. § 1526), which bars importation of merchandise bearing a counterfeit mark registered with the USPTO, and Section 42 of the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1124), which similarly prohibits importation of goods that copy or simulate a registered trademark. These statutes give CBP independent authority to seize infringing goods without a brand having to bring its own civil lawsuit against every individual shipment.

## Trademark Recordation: The Step That Actually Activates Enforcement

Owning a federal trademark registration is not, by itself, enough to trigger CBP border enforcement — a brand generally needs to separately record that registration with CBP through the agency's Intellectual Property Rights e-Recordation (IPRR) system. Recordation gives CBP officers a searchable database entry describing the mark, the goods it covers, and contact information for the rights holder, which is what allows front-line officers to identify a shipment as potentially infringing and act on it. Brands typically also submit product identification guides through IPRR to help officers distinguish authentic goods from counterfeits during physical inspection.

## What Happens When a Shipment Is Flagged

When CBP identifies merchandise it suspects bears a counterfeit mark, it can detain the shipment for further examination. If merchandise bears a mark identical to, or substantially indistinguishable from, a recorded genuine mark, CBP can seize it outright. For non-identical marks that still raise infringement concerns, CBP generally provides limited samples or information to the rights holder to help confirm counterfeit status before final action, subject to procedures intended to protect confidential importer information.

## Civil Penalties for Importers

Beyond seizure of the goods themselves, importers of counterfeit merchandise can face civil penalties under 19 U.S.C. § 1526(f), which authorizes penalties tied to the value the goods would have had if genuine — for a first violation, up to the manufacturer's suggested retail price the genuine goods would have carried, and higher for repeat violations. These penalties are separate from, and can be imposed in addition to, any forfeiture of the goods themselves.

## Criminal Exposure

Trafficking in counterfeit goods can also carry criminal liability under 18 U.S.C. § 2320, which applies to a person who knowingly traffics or attempts to traffic in goods bearing a counterfeit mark. Criminal enforcement is typically pursued by federal prosecutors working with agencies such as Homeland Security Investigations, and is generally reserved for larger-scale, knowing trafficking operations rather than isolated personal imports, though the statute itself is not limited to any particular scale of operation.

## De Minimis Personal Imports and the Small-Package Problem

A significant share of modern counterfeit fashion enforcement involves small individual parcels shipped directly to consumers, often through e-commerce platforms, rather than bulk commercial shipments. This shift has pushed CBP and rights holders toward greater reliance on data-driven targeting and platform-level cooperation, since screening every individual small package at the volume seen in cross-border e-commerce presents very different operational challenges than inspecting containerized commercial freight.

## What Brands Typically Do Beyond Recordation

Brand enforcement programs commonly combine CBP recordation with civil trademark litigation against larger counterfeiters, takedown requests to online marketplaces under each platform's own intellectual property policies, and, in some cases, coordination with international customs authorities under bilateral or multilateral enforcement arrangements, since counterfeit fashion goods frequently transit through multiple jurisdictions before reaching a final destination market.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does CBP automatically seize any counterfeit good it finds?** Not automatically in every case — CBP has procedures for detention, sampling, and verification, particularly where the mark on the goods is not identical to the recorded genuine mark.

**Can an importer get seized goods back?** Generally not once goods are confirmed counterfeit and forfeited, though importers have administrative and, in some cases, judicial avenues to contest a seizure or penalty.

**Is recording a trademark with the USPTO enough to trigger border enforcement?** No — a separate recordation with CBP through its e-Recordation system is generally required for CBP to actively screen for a specific mark.
