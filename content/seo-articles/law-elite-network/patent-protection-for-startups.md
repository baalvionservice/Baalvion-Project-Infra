# Patent Protection for Startups

A patent doesn't protect an idea — it protects a specific, claimed invention that meets legal requirements a founder rarely has time to learn before they need to act. For an early-stage company, the practical question usually isn't "should we get a patent" in the abstract, but when filing actually makes sense given the cost, the stage of the technology, and what's actually at stake if a competitor copies it. This guide covers the U.S. patent system specifically — filing options, patentability, timing, and ownership — as a starting point for that conversation with a patent attorney, not a substitute for one.

## What Patent Protection Actually Requires

To be patentable in the U.S., an invention generally must be:

- **Novel** — not already disclosed publicly, anywhere, before the effective filing date (35 U.S.C. § 102). The U.S. has a limited one-year grace period for disclosures made by the inventor (or derived from the inventor) before filing, but relying on that grace period is risky, since many other countries have no equivalent grace period at all — a public disclosure before filing can permanently forfeit patent rights outside the U.S.
- **Non-obvious** — not merely an obvious variation of what already exists to someone skilled in the relevant field (35 U.S.C. § 103).
- **Useful and patent-eligible subject matter** — the invention must have a specific, practical utility and fall within the categories of eligible subject matter, which is a genuinely complex area for software and business-method inventions in particular.

Because the U.S. is a **first-inventor-to-file** system (since the America Invents Act), the practical filing race matters: being first to invent something is not enough if someone else files first.

## Provisional vs. Non-Provisional Applications

Founders most often encounter two filing options:

- **A provisional application** (35 U.S.C. § 111(b)) establishes an early filing date and lets the applicant use "patent pending" status, but it is never examined and automatically expires 12 months after filing. It's generally faster and less expensive to prepare than a full application, which is why it's often used to lock in a priority date while a startup keeps developing the technology or raising funding.
- **A non-provisional application** (35 U.S.C. § 111(a)) is the application that actually gets examined by the USPTO and can mature into an issued patent. If a provisional was filed first, the non-provisional must generally be filed within 12 months to claim the benefit of the provisional's earlier filing date — miss that window, and the priority date is lost.

A provisional buys time, not protection by itself: no patent, and no enforceable right against a copier, exists until a non-provisional application is filed, examined, and — if allowed — issues.

## "File Early, Claim Broadly" — What That Actually Means

Filing early protects the priority date against later filers and public disclosures, but a provisional application is only as useful as what it actually discloses — it needs to describe the invention in enough technical detail to support the claims that will eventually be drafted in the non-provisional application, not just a product pitch or a marketing description. "Claiming broadly" refers to how the non-provisional application's claims (the numbered legal statements that define exactly what's protected) are drafted: claims that are too narrow can be easy for a competitor to design around, while claims that are too broad risk being rejected over existing technology (prior art). Getting this balance right is a large part of what a patent attorney's drafting work actually consists of — it is not a task most founders should attempt without one, given how much later enforcement value rides on the claim language.

## Deciding Whether Patent Protection Makes Sense

Not every startup technology is well suited to (or well protected by) a patent. Some practical questions that typically inform the decision:

- **Would a competitor need to copy something visible, or could they reach the same result a different way?** A patent only stops the specific claimed approach, not the underlying problem being solved.
- **Would a trade secret work better?** Some inventions — particularly internal processes that don't need to be disclosed to work — may be better protected by keeping them confidential (a patent, once granted, is a public document that discloses how the invention works) than by patenting them.
- **Does the timeline fit the business?** Patent examination often takes multiple years, which may or may not align with how long the underlying technology is likely to matter competitively.
- **Is there budget for the full lifecycle?** Beyond the initial filing, issued patents typically require ongoing maintenance fees to stay in force, and enforcing a patent against an infringer is a separate, often expensive, undertaking.

## Filing Outside the U.S.

A U.S. patent only protects against infringement in the U.S. A startup with international ambitions or competitors may need protection elsewhere, and the **Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT)** provides a streamlined way to preserve the option to file in most major markets — filing a single PCT application generally preserves the ability to pursue national-phase patents in participating countries for up to 30–31 months from the earliest priority date, without having to file separately in every country immediately. This buys time to evaluate which markets are actually worth the cost of pursuing before committing to it.

## Ownership: The Issue Founders Most Often Get Wrong

Under U.S. law, patent rights generally start with the individual inventor(s), not the company — a company owns an invention only if it's properly assigned in writing, or in narrower circumstances where an enforceable "hired to invent" obligation applies. Startups that don't have clean, signed invention-assignment agreements with every founder, employee, and contractor who might touch the technology can end up with a patent (or the right to file one) partially owned by someone who is no longer involved with the company — a problem that is far cheaper to prevent with paperwork at the outset than to fix later.
