# What Was Law Enforcement Like in 1900s America?

Policing at the turn of the 20th century looked closer to its 19th-century roots than to anything recognizable as modern law enforcement. Officers patrolled on foot or on horseback, reported in by pulling a lever on a street-corner call box, and had no fingerprint database, no radio, and no forensic lab to rely on. Over the following three decades, that started to change — not all at once, but through a series of specific reforms, technologies, and institutions that laid the foundation for 20th-century policing. This guide covers what law enforcement actually looked like in early 1900s America and how it began to evolve.

## Local Versus State Law Enforcement

Around 1900, American law enforcement was overwhelmingly local. Cities ran their own police departments, and counties were policed by an elected **sheriff** and their deputies — an office with roots in English common law and colonial America, and one that, unlike a police chief, was (and in most states still is) chosen directly by voters. Dedicated statewide police agencies were rare in this period; the Texas Rangers were an early exception, but the broader wave of state police forces largely followed later, in the 1900s–1920s, often established to patrol rural highways or respond to labor unrest that local sheriffs' departments weren't equipped to handle alone. There was no federal police force in the modern sense at all until 1908.

## City Police Departments

Municipal police departments in 1900 were organized around a **beat system**: officers were assigned a fixed foot-patrol route in their own neighborhood, which they walked repeatedly during a shift, becoming a familiar, recognizable presence to residents and merchants along the way. Department size, training, and professionalism varied enormously by city — some large-city departments in this era were also entangled with local political machines, which influenced hiring, promotion, and assignments in ways that later-era reformers specifically targeted.

## Sheriffs and Deputies

County sheriffs in 1900 handled a broad mix of duties beyond criminal law enforcement — serving civil court papers, running the county jail, and, in many rural counties, acting as the only law enforcement presence across a wide, sparsely populated area. In parts of the country, sheriffs could still call on a **posse comitatus** — a group of deputized local citizens — to help pursue a suspect or respond to an emergency, a practice with deep roots in American frontier law enforcement that was already declining in most places by the early 1900s.

## Early Policing Structures and Patrol Practices

Foot patrol was the default in cities; mounted (horse) patrol was common as well, especially for larger beats and crowd or traffic control. August Vollmer, who became Berkeley, California's town marshal in 1905 and its first police chief in 1909, is credited with some of the era's most consequential patrol innovations: he put Berkeley officers on bicycle patrol in 1910, motorcycle patrol in 1911, and patrol cars in 1913 — years ahead of most American departments — while also pushing for college-educated officers, centralized crime records, and what would become modern investigative method. Vollmer later became president of the International Association of Chiefs of Police in 1922 and used that platform to advocate the same reforms nationally.

## Communication Methods

Officers on patrol had no personal radio and, for most of this period, no personal telephone. Cities instead relied on networks of street-corner **call boxes** — locked telegraph (and later telephone) stations, widely supplied by the Gamewell company, whose systems were already installed in roughly 500 U.S. cities by 1890. A patrol officer would use a call box at set intervals to check in with headquarters or "pull the hook" to signal an alarm; headquarters could also flash a light on a nearby box to summon a specific officer back to a phone. Some of the earliest police telephone lines date to Albany, New York in 1877, connecting the mayor's office to district stations, but two-way radio dispatch to patrol cars — the technology that finally replaced the call box — didn't become practical and widespread until later in the 1920s and 1930s.

## Transportation

At the start of the century, officers moved on foot, on horseback, or, for longer county-level travel, by horse and wagon or rail. The automobile changed this within roughly two decades: departments like Vollmer's Berkeley force adopted patrol cars as early as 1913, and motorized patrol steadily displaced horses in most urban departments through the 1910s and 1920s, though rural sheriffs' departments — covering far larger, less-paved territory — made the transition more gradually.

## Identification and Investigation Methods

Criminal identification in 1900 relied primarily on the **Bertillon system**, a French method (adopted by the NYPD starting in 1896, with its own dedicated photo studio operating by 1897) that recorded a set of precise body measurements — skull width, foot length, and others — alongside front-and-profile photographs, the origin of the modern mugshot format. **Fingerprinting** began entering American law enforcement in the early 1900s as a more reliable alternative, since fingerprints are unique to an individual in a way Bertillon's measurements were not; New York State prisons began adopting fingerprint identification in 1903, and departments and prison systems around the country gradually phased out Bertillon measurements over the following two decades — New York had largely retired the system by 1920, even as it kept the photograph format Bertillon had introduced. Beyond identification, there was no organized forensic laboratory science comparable to a modern crime lab in this period; investigation relied heavily on witness testimony, informants, and an investigator's own experience and judgment.

## Courts and Criminal Procedure

State and local courts, not federal courts, handled the overwhelming majority of criminal prosecutions in this period, much as they still do today. Compared to modern criminal procedure, defendants in 1900 had markedly fewer constitutional protections as a practical matter — many of the specific procedural protections associated with the Bill of Rights (such as the right to appointed counsel in state criminal cases) were not applied to state courts until Supreme Court decisions decades later, in the mid-20th century. Prosecutors were typically local officials — a district attorney or state's attorney — elected within their county or judicial district, similar to the modern structure described in our guide to [how the U.S. legal system works](/how-the-us-legal-system-works/).

## Prisons and Jails

County jails held people awaiting trial and those serving short sentences, while convicted felons served longer sentences in state penitentiaries, which by 1900 already numbered in the dozens nationwide, many built decades earlier in the 19th century. In parts of the South during this era, **convict leasing** — a system where state and local governments leased incarcerated people's labor to private businesses — was still practiced by some jurisdictions, a historically documented and widely criticized practice tied closely to the racial dynamics of the post-Reconstruction South; it was phased out at different times state by state over the following decades.

## Urban Versus Rural Policing

Urban departments had more officers, more hierarchy, and — especially after Vollmer-style reforms began spreading — earlier access to new technology and formal training. Rural law enforcement remained comparatively sparse: a county sheriff and a small number of deputies might be responsible for policing an enormous geographic area with limited resources, relying more heavily on community knowledge, informal networks, and, when necessary, a deputized posse, well into the 20th century.

## Major Technological and Institutional Changes

Several developments defined this era's evolution in law enforcement:

- **1893** — Police chiefs from around the country founded what became the International Association of Chiefs of Police, initially organized to help agencies share information about suspects who crossed jurisdictional lines.
- **1896–1903** — Criminal identification shifted from the Bertillon measurement system toward fingerprinting, starting with New York's state prison system.
- **1905–1913** — August Vollmer's tenure as Berkeley's police chief introduced bicycle, motorcycle, and automobile patrol, along with an early push for officer education and centralized records.
- **1908** — U.S. Attorney General Charles Bonaparte created a dedicated force of federal investigators within the Department of Justice; renamed the Bureau of Investigation in 1909, it became the Federal Bureau of Investigation in 1935.

## Historical Limitations and Challenges

Law enforcement in this period operated with limited formal training, no standardized national hiring or certification requirements, and significant local political influence over hiring and discipline in many cities. Forensic science as a reliable investigative tool was in its infancy. Legal protections for suspects that are taken for granted today had not yet been established. And oversight mechanisms — internal affairs functions, civilian review, and the body of constitutional criminal-procedure case law that constrains police conduct today — were largely undeveloped or nonexistent.

## How Early Law Enforcement Differs From Modern Policing

The clearest throughline from 1900 to today is professionalization: the shift from a foot-patrol, call-box, politically-influenced model toward standardized training, motorized and radio-dispatched patrol, forensic identification, and a body of constitutional case law governing police conduct. Much of that shift began, in identifiable form, in the exact period this guide covers — Vollmer's Berkeley reforms, the early fingerprinting transition, and the 1908 creation of a federal investigative force were each early steps in a professionalization process that continued, in further stages, for decades afterward.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What was law enforcement like in 1900s America?** Overwhelmingly local and low-tech by modern standards: city police on foot or horseback patrol, county sheriffs handling rural areas, communication through street-corner call boxes rather than radio, and identification methods (the Bertillon system, later fingerprinting) that were only beginning to modernize.

**Did police have cars in the early 1900s?** Not at first. Motorized patrol began appearing in the 1910s — Berkeley, California adopted patrol cars as early as 1913 under Chief August Vollmer — and spread gradually to other departments through the 1910s and 1920s.

**When did fingerprinting start being used by U.S. police?** New York's state prison system began adopting fingerprint identification in 1903, gradually replacing the older Bertillon measurement system over the following two decades.

**Was there a federal police force in 1900?** No. The first dedicated federal investigative force was created within the Department of Justice in 1908; it was renamed the Bureau of Investigation in 1909 and became the FBI in 1935.

**How did rural policing differ from city policing in this era?** Rural areas were typically policed by an elected county sheriff and a small number of deputies covering a large territory with limited resources, while city departments had more officers, more structure, and earlier access to new patrol technology and training reforms.
