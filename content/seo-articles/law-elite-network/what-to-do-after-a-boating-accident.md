# What to Do After a Boating Accident

The moments after a boating accident are often chaotic, and it is not always obvious what to do first. This guide walks through the practical steps that generally matter most — safety, reporting, evidence, medical care, and protecting your legal options — in a general, sensible order. It is not a substitute for individualized legal or medical advice, and safety should always come before any of the steps described here.

## 1. Get to Safety First

Before anything else, focus on getting everyone out of immediate danger. That may mean moving away from a sinking or capsized vessel, getting swimmers out of a boat traffic lane, or moving an injured person to a stable position if it is safe to do so. Do not attempt rescues or maneuvers that put you or others at further risk — wait for on-water rescue personnel if conditions are unsafe.

## 2. Call for Emergency Help When Necessary

If anyone is injured, missing, or in the water, or if a vessel is taking on water or on fire, contact 911 or hail the U.S. Coast Guard on marine VHF radio channel 16 as appropriate for your location. Do not delay emergency calls to gather information first — safety comes first.

## 3. Assist Injured People When It Is Safe to Do So

If you are able to safely provide basic first aid, do so, but avoid moving someone with a suspected spinal or serious injury unless it is necessary to prevent further harm (for example, getting them out of the water). Let trained responders take over once they arrive.

## 4. Report the Accident When Legally Required

Boating accidents that meet certain thresholds — generally involving death, an injury requiring medical treatment beyond first aid, a person's disappearance, or property damage above a set dollar amount, or a complete loss of a vessel — must be reported to the appropriate state boating authority or the U.S. Coast Guard, typically within a set timeframe after the accident (often within 48 hours for death, serious injury, or disappearance, and a longer window for property-damage-only accidents). Reporting requirements and exact timeframes can vary somewhat by state, so if you are unsure whether your accident must be reported, it is generally safer to report it or ask local authorities than to assume it does not qualify.

Federal regulations set general reporting thresholds and timeframes that many state boating authorities follow closely: under 33 C.F.R. § 173.55, a report is generally required when an accident involves a death, an injury requiring medical treatment beyond first aid, a person's disappearance, or property damage totaling $2,000 or more (or the complete loss of a vessel). Reports involving death, injury requiring treatment beyond first aid, or disappearance are generally due within 48 hours, while property-damage-only reports are generally due within 10 days. Exact state procedures can vary, so confirm requirements with your state boating authority if you are unsure.

## 5. Exchange Information With Other Parties

Where it is safe and practical, exchange names, contact information, vessel registration numbers, and insurance information with any other operators or owners involved. If a rental or charter company is involved, note the company's name and contact details as well.

## 6. Photograph and Video the Scene When Possible

If you are able to do so safely, photos and video of the vessels involved, visible damage, the surrounding water and weather conditions, and any visible injuries can be valuable later. Try to capture the overall scene as well as close-up detail, and note the time and general location.

## 7. Identify Witnesses

Other boaters, passengers, marina staff, or people on shore may have seen what happened. If possible, note their names and contact information at the scene, since witnesses can be difficult to locate later.

## 8. Preserve Physical Evidence

If it is safe and lawful to do so, avoid repairing or discarding damaged equipment, safety gear, or vessel components until you have had a chance to document their condition or discuss the accident with an attorney. Physical evidence can be important in determining what caused the accident.

## 9. Seek Medical Attention Promptly

Some injuries — including head injuries and internal injuries — are not immediately obvious. Seeking medical evaluation promptly, even if you feel you have avoided serious injury, is generally a good practice for your health and helps create a medical record connecting any injury to the accident.

## 10. Keep Complete Medical Records

Follow through on recommended treatment and keep records of every visit, diagnosis, and provider recommendation. Significant, unexplained gaps in treatment can later be used to question whether an injury was as serious as claimed, so consistent follow-through matters.

## 11. Notify the Appropriate Insurer Carefully

You will generally need to notify your own insurer that an accident occurred, since most policies require timely notice as a condition of coverage. When speaking with any insurer — including your own — stick to basic facts (what happened, when, and where) and be cautious about providing a detailed recorded statement, speculating about fault, or agreeing to a settlement before understanding the full extent of your injuries and legal options.

## 12. Avoid Speculative Statements About Fault

Immediately after an accident, the full picture is often unclear — visibility, mechanical issues, and the other party's conduct may not be fully known yet. Avoid guessing at or apologizing in a way that could be read as admitting fault, and stick to factual observations when speaking with authorities, insurers, or other parties involved.

## 13. Preserve Communications and Documents

Keep copies of any accident report, correspondence with insurers, rental or charter agreements, receipts, and any other documents related to the accident and your recovery. Organized records make it easier for an attorney to evaluate a potential claim later.

## 14. Understand That Deadlines May Apply

Boating accident claims are subject to filing deadlines that vary depending on the state, the type of claim, and whether federal maritime law is involved. These deadlines can be shorter than people expect, and missing one can permanently bar a claim. Our guide to [boating accident statutes of limitations](/boating-accident-statute-of-limitations/) explains this in more detail — but because the applicable deadline depends on the specific facts, it should be confirmed with a qualified attorney rather than assumed.

## 15. Consider Speaking With a Qualified Attorney

Especially where an accident involves significant injury, a disputed account of what happened, multiple vessels, a rental or charter company, or an insurer that is slow to respond, a consultation with a [boating accident lawyer](/boating-accident-lawyer/) can help clarify your options before deadlines pass or evidence is lost. Many boating accident attorneys offer an initial consultation to discuss the facts of a case.

## A Quick-Reference Checklist

- Prioritize safety and emergency care above all else
- Call for help and report the accident where required
- Exchange information with other parties involved
- Photograph the scene, damage, and injuries if safe to do so
- Identify and note contact information for witnesses
- Preserve damaged equipment and safety gear
- Get a prompt medical evaluation, even for seemingly minor injuries
- Keep complete, consistent medical records
- Notify your insurer, but avoid speculating about fault
- Keep copies of all accident-related documents and communications
- Be aware that filing deadlines apply and can vary by state
- Consider consulting a [boating accident lawyer](/boating-accident-lawyer/) if the accident involved significant injury or a dispute over what happened

## Understanding Fault Comes Later

It is common, and normal, not to fully understand who was at fault immediately after an accident. Questions about operator conduct, vessel maintenance, and shared responsibility are often sorted out over time as evidence is gathered. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I have to report every boating accident?** No. Reporting requirements generally apply once certain thresholds are met, such as injury, death, disappearance, or property damage above a set amount. When in doubt, contact the relevant state boating authority to confirm.

**Should I give the other party's insurer a recorded statement right away?** Be cautious. You generally are not obligated to give a detailed recorded statement to another party's insurer, and it is often worth discussing this with an attorney first, particularly for anything beyond a serious injury.

**What if I don't have photos or witness information?** It is still worth pursuing a claim if warranted; missing information can sometimes be reconstructed through accident reports, medical records, and other investigation, though earlier evidence preservation is always preferable.

**How soon should I contact a lawyer?** There is no fixed rule, but because evidence can be lost and some deadlines are shorter than expected, contacting an attorney sooner rather than later is generally advisable for any accident involving significant injury or a dispute about fault.

**What if the accident involved a rental boat or jet ski?** Note the rental company's name, location, and any rental or waiver paperwork you signed, and keep a copy if possible. Rental and charter companies can have their own legal obligations separate from the individual operator, which our [boating accident lawyer guide](/boating-accident-lawyer/) discusses further.
