# How Contingency Fee Agreements Work

A contingency fee is a way of paying a lawyer that depends on the outcome of a case: instead of billing by the hour, the lawyer's fee is a percentage of whatever is recovered through settlement or verdict, and generally nothing is owed for attorney's fees if there is no recovery. This arrangement is especially common in personal injury cases, including car accident, boating accident, maritime, and cruise ship injury claims. This guide explains how contingency arrangements generally work, what they typically include, and what to look for before signing one. It does not describe any specific fee, percentage, or arrangement offered by any particular lawyer.

## What a Contingency Fee Is

A contingency fee agreement is a written contract between a lawyer and a client under which the lawyer's fee is contingent — dependent — on a successful outcome. If the case settles or wins at trial, the lawyer's fee is calculated as an agreed-upon percentage of the amount recovered. If the case is unsuccessful, the client generally does not owe attorney's fees, though, as discussed below, case costs and expenses may be treated differently.

## How the Fee Generally Works

In a typical contingency arrangement:

1. The lawyer and client sign a written agreement before work begins, setting out the fee percentage and how it applies.
2. The lawyer advances the work — investigation, negotiation, and, if necessary, litigation — without charging the client hourly.
3. If the case resolves through settlement or judgment, the agreed percentage is deducted from the recovery before the remaining funds are disbursed to the client.
4. If the case does not result in a recovery, the client generally does not pay an attorney's fee, though the treatment of costs already spent on the case can vary by agreement.

## Why Personal Injury Lawyers Commonly Use Contingency Arrangements

Contingency fees allow injured people to obtain legal representation without paying an hourly rate they may not be able to afford, particularly while dealing with medical bills, lost income, or other financial strain following an accident. The arrangement also aligns the lawyer's incentive with the client's: the lawyer is generally motivated to maximize the recovery, since the fee is tied directly to it. This model is most common in personal injury, and less common in areas of law where outcomes are not measured in a monetary recovery, such as many family law or criminal defense matters, which are more typically billed hourly or as a flat fee.

## Attorney Fees Versus Case Expenses

It is important to distinguish between two different categories of cost in a contingency-fee case:

- **Attorney's fees** — the percentage-based payment to the lawyer for their work, contingent on recovery.
- **Case expenses** — out-of-pocket costs incurred while pursuing the claim, such as filing fees, costs of obtaining medical records, expert witness fees, deposition costs, and investigation expenses.

Whether case expenses are advanced by the lawyer and reimbursed only out of a successful recovery, charged to the client regardless of outcome, or handled some other way, depends entirely on the specific written agreement. This is one of the most important — and most variable — terms to understand before signing.

## Percentage-Based Fees

Contingency fees are calculated as a percentage of the amount recovered. That percentage is not standardized across the legal profession: it can vary based on the lawyer, the type of case, the jurisdiction, the complexity of the matter, and the specific agreement negotiated. Some agreements also apply a different percentage depending on the stage at which the case resolves, discussed next.

**There is no single, universal contingency fee percentage that applies to every case, lawyer, or state.** Any specific percentage should be confirmed by reading the actual written fee agreement being offered.

## Settlement Versus Trial Arrangements

Some contingency fee agreements apply a single flat percentage regardless of how or when the case resolves. Others use a tiered or "sliding scale" structure, where the percentage may increase if the case proceeds past a certain stage — for example, if a lawsuit must be filed, or if the case proceeds to trial or appeal — reflecting the additional time, risk, and resources involved. Whether a particular agreement uses a flat rate or a tiered structure, and where the thresholds fall, varies by lawyer and by agreement.

## How the Agreement Should Be Documented

Contingency fee agreements should always be in writing. Written fee agreements are standard practice in the legal profession and are required, in various forms, under professional-conduct rules discussed below. A written agreement should clearly state:

- The percentage (or tiered percentages) that will be charged
- How the percentage is calculated — for example, whether it is calculated before or after case expenses are deducted from the recovery
- How case expenses are handled, including what happens if there is no recovery
- What work is covered by the agreement, and what might fall outside it
- How the client can end the representation, and how fees are handled if that happens

## What Happens if the Case Is Unsuccessful

In a typical contingency arrangement, if the case does not result in a settlement or verdict, the client does not owe an attorney's fee. However, "no recovery, no fee" language usually refers specifically to the attorney's fee — it does not automatically mean every possible case expense is waived. Some agreements make the client responsible for repaying advanced costs even without a recovery; others absorb that risk entirely. This distinction is one of the most important things to clarify before signing.

## Costs That May or May Not Be Included

Depending on the agreement, case-related expenses can include items such as court filing fees, fees for obtaining medical or employment records, expert witness fees, deposition and court reporter costs, investigation or accident-reconstruction costs, and postage or copying charges. Because these costs can add up, especially in cases that proceed to litigation, it is worth asking specifically how they will be handled — and getting the answer in writing — rather than assuming.

## Medical Liens and Other Potential Deductions

In addition to attorney's fees and case expenses, a settlement or verdict in a personal injury case can be subject to other deductions before the client receives their net recovery. These can include liens from health insurers, government health programs, or medical providers who treated the injury on the understanding they would be paid from the eventual recovery. Whether such liens exist, and how they are resolved, depends on the specific facts of the case, the client's insurance situation, and applicable state and federal law. A lawyer typically identifies and addresses potential liens as part of finalizing a settlement, since resolving them incorrectly can create problems for the client later.

## Questions to Ask Before Signing

- What percentage will you charge, and does it change if the case goes to litigation or trial?
- Is the percentage calculated before or after case expenses are deducted?
- How are case expenses handled if there is no recovery?
- What specific expenses might I be responsible for?
- Are there any circumstances where I could owe money out of pocket?
- How and when will I receive an accounting of fees, costs, and any liens at the end of the case?

## Why Fee Agreements Vary

Fee agreements are not uniform because cases are not uniform. A straightforward claim with clear liability and cooperative insurance carries different time, cost, and risk than a disputed case likely to require litigation, expert testimony, or an appeal. Jurisdiction matters too: some states cap contingency fees in certain types of cases (for example, some states limit fees in medical malpractice claims), while others leave the percentage to negotiation between lawyer and client, subject to a general requirement that the fee be reasonable. Because of this variation, the same type of case can reasonably carry different fee terms from different lawyers or in different states.

## How to Compare Fee Agreements

When comparing offers from more than one lawyer, look beyond the headline percentage. Compare how expenses are handled, whether the percentage changes at different case stages, what happens if you end the representation early, and how clearly the agreement is written. A lower percentage paired with a client-unfriendly expense clause is not necessarily a better deal than a different structure with clearer, more favorable expense terms — the full agreement matters, not just one number.

## Professional-Conduct Rules

Contingency fees are governed by professional-conduct rules that vary by state, but most states' rules are modeled in some form on the American Bar Association's Model Rules of Professional Conduct, Rule 1.5. That rule requires, among other things, that a lawyer's fee be reasonable, and that a contingency fee agreement be in writing, signed by the client, and state the method for determining the fee — including percentages that apply at each stage of the matter and how expenses will be deducted. Some states also prohibit contingency fees entirely in certain matters, such as most family law and criminal defense cases. Because these rules differ by state, the specific requirements that apply to a given agreement should be confirmed against that state's rules of professional conduct.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do all personal injury lawyers charge the same contingency percentage?** No. Percentages vary by lawyer, case type, jurisdiction, and the specific terms negotiated. There is no single standard rate across the industry.

**Will I ever have to pay out of pocket?** It depends on the agreement. Some agreements make the client responsible for case expenses even without a recovery; others do not. This should be clarified in writing before signing.

**Can the percentage change during the case?** Some agreements use a single flat rate; others increase the percentage if the case proceeds to litigation or trial. This should be spelled out clearly in the written agreement.

**What if I'm unhappy with my lawyer and want to switch?** Most agreements address what happens to fees if representation ends before the case resolves. This is worth understanding before signing, not after a dispute arises.

**Are liens deducted before or after the attorney's fee?** This depends on how the specific settlement and fee agreement are structured, and can also depend on the type of lien involved. A lawyer typically walks through this at the time of settlement.

**Is a contingency fee agreement negotiable?** In many cases, yes — fee terms are generally a matter of agreement between lawyer and client, subject to the requirement that the fee be reasonable and consistent with applicable professional-conduct rules.
