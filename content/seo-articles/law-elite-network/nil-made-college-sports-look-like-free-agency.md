# How College Sports Quietly Turned Into Free Agency

Until July 1, 2021, a college quarterback could sell out a stadium every Saturday, appear on national television, have his jersey sold in the campus bookstore — and if he took a single dollar for it, the NCAA could strip his eligibility. Everyone else in the building was getting paid: the coach, the athletic director, the TV network, the school. The athlete generating the actual attention wasn't allowed to touch a cent of it. Then, almost overnight, that entire rule just... stopped existing.

## It didn't change because the NCAA wanted it to

This is the part that gets lost in most explanations: the NCAA didn't have a change of heart. It lost. A string of legal challenges, most notably *NCAA v. Alston* at the Supreme Court, chipped away at the argument that "amateurism" justified restricting athlete compensation, and by mid-2021 several states had already passed their own laws saying college athletes in their state could earn NIL money starting on a specific date, with or without the NCAA's permission. The NCAA's own rulebook was about to become unenforceable in a growing number of states regardless of what it decided, so it adopted an interim policy allowing NIL nationally rather than watch its authority get carved up state by state.

That's why the shift felt so sudden. It wasn't a considered policy rollout — it was an organization getting outrun by its own legal exposure and issuing an emergency fix.

## What actually got unlocked

Once the interim policy landed, athletes could sign endorsement deals, monetize social media, get paid for autograph signings and appearances — all the ordinary stuff athletes at the professional level had always been able to do. What nobody fully anticipated was how fast a second thing formed around it: NIL collectives. These are essentially booster-funded pools, loosely tied to a specific school, whose entire purpose is paying that school's athletes for "NIL activities" that often look, from the outside, less like an endorsement deal and more like a straightforward payment to play there.

The NCAA still technically bans paying someone to enroll or stay enrolled — that's supposed to be the line between a real endorsement and disguised pay-for-play. In practice, that line has been almost impossible to police, because a collective can always point to some appearance or autograph session as the "consideration" for the payment. Whether that's a real distinction or a fig leaf is genuinely contested, and it's the main reason the system looks less like an endorsement market and more like schools competing on payroll.

## The transfer portal made it look even more like a league

NIL landed right around the same time the NCAA was also loosening its transfer rules, letting athletes switch schools without sitting out a year. Put those two changes together and you get something that functions a lot like free agency: an athlete can now enter the transfer portal, and schools' NIL collectives can effectively compete for that athlete with money, the same way pro franchises compete for free agents — except there's no salary cap, no league office setting rules, and no players' union negotiating on the athletes' behalf the way there would be in a real professional league.

## Where it's actually headed now

The *House v. NCAA* settlement pushed things a step further, moving toward schools being allowed to pay athletes directly out of school revenue rather than relying entirely on outside collectives — which is a meaningfully different model, closer to how a professional team pays its own roster instead of players earning money from third-party sponsors. That shift raises its own questions schools are still working through: Title IX implications of how revenue-sharing gets distributed between men's and women's programs, how a school allocates a limited pool across dozens of sports, and whether this eventually forces some kind of formal employment relationship between athletes and their universities — something courts and the Department of Labor have circled around for years without fully resolving.

## The part that's easy to miss

None of this created a level playing field — it mostly reshuffled who has the advantage. A blue-blood program with a wealthy, well-organized booster network can now out-collective almost anyone, in a way that has very little to do with coaching or facilities and a lot to do with which alumni base can raise money fastest. The athletes benefiting most tend to be at programs that were already recruiting powerhouses. What NIL actually did wasn't level college sports — it just made the money that was always flowing through the system finally visible, and let the people generating it get a cut, however unevenly that cut ends up getting distributed.
