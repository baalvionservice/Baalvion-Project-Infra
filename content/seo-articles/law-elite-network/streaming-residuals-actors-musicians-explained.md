# Why Your Favorite Show Being a Hit on Netflix Doesn't Mean the Actors Got Rich

Here's a question a lot of people assume has an obvious answer and don't realize is actually strange: if a show is one of the most-watched things on Netflix for months, do the actors in it get paid more? For decades, the answer for TV was yes — a hit show meant reruns, and reruns meant residual checks, sometimes for years. Streaming quietly broke that entirely, and most people never noticed until the actors did, loudly, in 2023.

## The old system actually worked pretty well

Before streaming, a network show's afterlife looked like this: it aired once, then it got syndicated — sold into reruns on other channels, sold overseas, eventually landed on basic cable at 2am forever. Every one of those re-airings triggered a residual payment to the actors, writers, and directors, calculated off actual viewership and licensing revenue. A genuinely popular show could mean real, ongoing income for someone who played a recurring character for two seasons a decade earlier. This is a big part of how character actors historically built a stable career instead of living gig to gig.

## Streaming has no reruns, so it built a system with no reruns baked in

A show on Netflix doesn't get "sold into syndication." It just... sits there, and people watch it whenever, as many times as they want, and Netflix doesn't publicly report exactly how many times. When streaming originals started in the 2010s, residual formulas for them were negotiated as flat payments — a fixed fee tied loosely to the platform's subscriber count at the time, paid once or twice, regardless of whether the show became a global phenomenon or nobody watched it. A supporting actor on a show that became one of the most-watched things in Netflix history and a supporting actor on a show that got quietly cancelled after one season could, under the old formula, end up with nearly identical residual checks.

That's not a hypothetical — it's the specific thing SAG-AFTRA pointed to during the 2023 strike, and it's a big part of why the strike happened at all.

## What actually changed in 2023

The deal that ended the SAG-AFTRA strike introduced something new: a streaming participation bonus, tied to whether a show actually performs well on a platform, not just to subscriber counts at release. It's still not a direct per-view residual the way old-school reruns were — streamers still don't publish exact viewership the way Nielsen used to measure broadcast — but it was the first real move toward "if this show is genuinely popular, the people in it get paid more for that," instead of a flat fee decided in advance no matter what happens after release.

It's worth being honest that this didn't fully solve the problem. It's a bonus layered on top of an already-lower base structure, not a return to the old percentage-of-revenue model, and plenty of actors and their reps still argue it doesn't come close to matching what a genuinely hit show would have generated under the syndication-era system.

## Music streaming has a version of this same problem, for a different reason

Musicians dealing with Spotify and Apple Music aren't fighting the "no reruns" problem — they're fighting a pool-splitting problem. Here's the part that surprises people: streaming services don't pay a fixed amount per stream. They take total subscription revenue for the month, put it in one big pool, and divide it based on what share of all streams on the platform that month your song represents. Your song's payout literally depends on how many other songs were streamed that same month — the "per-stream rate" everyone quotes (often cited around $0.003–$0.005) is really just an average, not a set price, and it moves depending on total platform activity.

This is a big reason a song can have millions of streams and still generate what looks like a strangely small check, especially once it's split between the artist, songwriter, producer, and whoever owns the master recording — often not the artist themselves, especially for anyone signed to a label under an older-style deal.

## So who's actually being paid, and how much say do they have

For actors, whether streaming pay feels fair often comes down to how much leverage they had when the deal was signed — a lead actor with real negotiating power can often get profit participation or a much stronger guarantee than a supporting or background actor working under standard scale. For musicians, it comes down to who owns the master and who owns the publishing — an artist on an independent deal keeping their masters is often in a fundamentally different position than one signed decades ago under a contract that assigned masters to the label for the life of copyright, long before streaming existed as a concept.

Neither of these is really a story about streaming being inherently unfair to creative work. It's more that an entire payment system was built for a world where content had a physical release window and a syndication afterlife, and when that world disappeared, the industry backfilled a new system under real time pressure — and that backfill is still, genuinely, being fought over.
