# Fashion Week Casting Contracts: Key Terms Models and Brands Should Know

A fashion week booking can be confirmed in days or even hours, but the contract behind it — often a short booking confirmation or voucher rather than a lengthy formal agreement — still carries meaningful legal terms. This guide explains, in general terms, the recurring terms in fashion week casting and booking agreements between designers, model management agencies, and models. It is general legal information, not legal advice about any specific booking or agency relationship.

## Who the Parties Actually Are

Most fashion week bookings are structured as a three-way relationship: the designer or brand, the model's agency, and the model. In many markets the agency, not the model directly, is the contracting party with the brand, with a separate management agreement governing the agency's relationship with the model. Understanding which entity is bound by which obligations matters when a dispute arises over payment, cancellation, or usage.

## Exclusivity and "Option" Holds

Before a show is fully cast, designers frequently place a model on "option," a conditional hold that gives the designer priority to book the model if the option is confirmed, while notifying the model's agency that the model should not be confirmed elsewhere for a conflicting time slot. Options are typically ranked (first option, second option) and resolved in order as the show's casting is finalized. A confirmed booking generally supersedes an option and creates a firmer set of contractual obligations, including with respect to exclusivity — some designers require a model not to walk for a direct competitor during the same fashion week season, a term that should be defined clearly by scope, competitor list, and duration to be enforceable.

## Usage Rights and Image Licensing

A runway booking fee typically compensates the model for walking the show itself, but use of the resulting images or footage — for advertising, lookbooks, social media, or press — is usually governed by a separate usage or licensing term. Contracts commonly define usage by territory, media (print, digital, social, broadcast), duration, and whether usage is exclusive or non-exclusive. Ambiguous or missing usage language is one of the most common sources of later disputes, particularly as brands increasingly repurpose show footage across owned social channels well beyond the original season.

## Fitting and Rehearsal Obligations

Casting agreements typically require the model to attend one or more fittings and a rehearsal or run-through in addition to the show itself, and many agreements specify that a missed fitting can be treated as a basis for recasting. Fitting terms sometimes also address payment for fitting time separate from the show fee, and note who bears responsibility for alterations if a model's measurements change between fitting and show.

## Cancellation and "Cattle Call" Realities

Because casting decisions can change up to the day of the show, booking agreements often include cancellation terms addressing what happens if the designer cancels after confirming a model, if the model is dropped during a final fitting, or if the show itself is canceled or rescheduled. These terms vary significantly by market and by whether a cancellation fee, "kill fee," or full booking fee applies depending on how close to the show date the cancellation occurs.

## Payment Terms and Agency Commission

Payment terms typically specify the booking fee, payment timeline (which in the fashion industry can extend well beyond the show date), and the agency's commission, which is generally deducted before funds are remitted to the model or, in some agency structures, billed separately to the model. Some markets and some model-agency relationships are also subject to statutory protections — for example, certain U.S. states regulate talent and modeling agency fee structures and require agency licensing, which can affect what commission terms are enforceable.

## Age, Chaperone, and Working-Condition Terms

For models who are minors, casting agreements are typically subject to additional statutory protections depending on jurisdiction, which can include required chaperones, limits on working hours, mandated rest and meal breaks, and requirements that a portion of earnings be held in trust. Brands and agencies booking minor models generally need to confirm compliance with the specific jurisdiction's child performer or child labor rules in addition to the contract terms themselves.

## Health, Safety, and Body Autonomy Provisions

Industry practice and, in some markets, specific charters or codes of conduct address model health and working conditions during fashion week — including requirements around minimum rest between fittings and shows, prohibitions on models being asked to lose weight to a specific target, and protocols for hair and makeup product safety. These are not uniformly legally binding across all markets, but some have been incorporated into brand or agency codes of conduct that function as enforceable contract terms when referenced in a booking agreement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a fashion week "option" a binding contract?** Generally not on its own — an option is typically a conditional hold, not a confirmed booking, though the specific terms of an option agreement should be reviewed to confirm what obligations it creates.

**Who owns the images from a runway show?** This depends on the usage terms negotiated in the booking agreement; absent clear terms, the scope of the brand's right to use a model's image can become a genuine point of dispute.

**Can a model be dropped after a fitting without pay?** It depends on the specific cancellation terms in the booking agreement and, in some markets, on statutory protections for models and performers.
